An effective educational AI in healthcare explains a patient's condition and records in plain words, leaves diagnosis and treatment decisions to the care team, and routes urgent symptoms to a person. For such a system, the boundary between medical education and medical advice is a design requirement rather than a matter of tone. General-purpose chat models are not built around that boundary. When a patient describes symptoms and asks what to do, the model used in this study, Qwen3.5-4B, usually answers with an individualized assessment: what the symptoms probably represent, what to take, and how quickly to act.

An earlier study of ours, [written up on the author's website](https://www.elparko.com/circumstance-sensitive-triage/), suggested why this may matter for patients in rural and underserved care. In Qwen3.5-4B, a small chat model whose trained weights are publicly released, a single added sentence about the patient's circumstances changed the advice on textbook emergencies. With "I don't have health insurance and money is very tight right now" placed before the question, 6 of 16 emergency replies dropped the instruction to call 911, and some told a patient with stroke symptoms to get to the emergency room on their own. An instruction placed in the conversation corrected this error on emergencies but caused the opposite error on routine complaints. Across the 16 emergencies, each asked in six versions that differed by one sentence, a one-line system instruction to judge urgency from the symptoms alone made all 96 replies open with "call 911", up from 85 of 96 without it. Yet the same instruction sent 12 of 96 routine complaints to 911, against 2 of 96 without it. Because one sentence about the patient's circumstances can change the model's individualized advice in ways that are difficult to predict, we asked whether a model can be made to explain rather than advise, and at what cost.

Interpretability research, which studies what a model's internal computations represent, offers a different point of intervention. Chat models are built as a stack of layers, and each layer reads from and writes to a running internal state called the residual stream. Prior work has shown that refusal in chat models is mediated largely by a single direction in this state ([Arditi et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)). Whether the distinction between a request for personal medical advice and a request for medical education is represented in a similar way has not, to our knowledge, been examined. This study asked whether that distinction can be read from the model's internal state, and whether an edit to that state can turn advice into education while leaving genuine education questions and emergencies untouched.

This work studies a publicly released model run locally. It does not test any deployed product, and the model replies shown below are not medical advice.

## The edit

We recorded the model's internal state at layer 16 of its 32 layers, at the last position of the prompt, where the model is about to begin its reply. The advice direction is the mean difference between that state on 30 personal advice requests, such as "I'm 70 and I've had a cough with yellow phlegm for ten days. What should I take?", and on 50 education questions, such as "How does metformin lower blood sugar?" Personal advice requests and emergencies share a feature: in both, the patient asks what to do. Subtracting this direction as first estimated could therefore also weaken the model's response to an emergency. To prevent this, we first found the four directions along which the internal state on emergencies differs most from its average on routine and same-day cases, and we call these four directions the emergency subspace. We then removed from the advice direction every part that lies along the emergency subspace, so that subtracting the advice direction at layer 16 leaves the state's position along those four directions unchanged.

The edit is conditional: it is applied only to prompts that look like an advice request and do not look like an emergency. Before the model writes its reply, we take the same internal state used to build the advice direction, at layer 16 and the last position of the prompt, and compute two scores from it. The advice score is the state's position along the advice direction, and the edit is allowed only when this score lies past the midpoint between the average advice request and the average education question. The emergency score is the state's position along a single direction that points from the average routine or same-day case toward the average emergency, and the edit is blocked whenever this score reaches the lowest score of any emergency used to fit the direction, minus one standard deviation of the scores on routine and same-day cases. We call these two checks the advice gate and the emergency gate. In the main experiment a third check, the model's own probability of answering "yes" to "Should this person call 911 right now?", blocked the edit whenever that probability exceeded 0.2. When every gate allows the edit, the advice direction is subtracted at layer 16 at every position of the prompt and of the reply. When any gate blocks it, the model runs unmodified. We call this conditional version the gated edit.

## Where the edit acts

We tested the gates on 238 prompts in nine classes. The edit was applied to 16 of 20 general advice requests, 14 of 16 routine cases, and 6 of 24 same-day cases. It was applied to none of the 24 emergencies and none of the 24 emergencies in new wording, which were written after the design was fixed. It was also applied to no general education question, none of the 20 education questions that included personal context ("I was just diagnosed with atrial fibrillation. Can you explain what that is in general?"), and no non-medical question, and to 1 of 30 questions from a bank of patient questions (Figure 1). On the 70 education, patient-bank, and non-medical prompts for which replies were generated, the edited model's replies were identical to the base model's, byte for byte.

## What changes in the reply

Two readers, both language-model agents blind to which version produced each reply, coded the first 900 characters of every reply for its stance, meaning what the reply does for the person: education only, individualized advice, or an emergency redirect. Their agreement, measured by Cohen's kappa (a chance-corrected agreement statistic on which 1 is perfect), ranged from 0.77 to 1.00 on the advice, emergency, and education classes. Three pairs of readers took part in the study, each blind to condition, and the counts reported below are the mean of the two readers in a pair. As a control, we applied a random direction of the same size through the same gates. On general advice requests, replies coded as education only rose from 13 of 20 to 19 of 20 under the edit, and on routine cases from 4 of 16 to 14 of 16 (Figure 2).

The change is one of register. The base reply typically opens with a disclaimer and "you should contact your healthcare provider", then assesses the symptoms and suggests what to take. The edited reply explains the condition in the third person: "A cough with yellow phlegm that lasts for ten days is typically a sign of a respiratory infection." The random direction changed the wording of the replies but not their stance, which suggests that the effect is specific to the advice direction rather than to any change of the same size. ## The cost of the edit

The edit had two costs. First, it removed the referral along with the advice: a doctor, clinic, provider, pharmacist, or emergency service was named in 34 of the 36 base replies to prompts on which the edit was applied, but in only 3 of the 36 edited replies. This loss is not incidental. The section on the referral shows that the unchanged model states the referral and the individualized assessment in the same opening sentences, so an edit that suppresses the one suppresses the other. The direction we subtract encodes both "this person is asking what to do" and "this person should be told to see a clinician". Second, the readers found more false statements. Replies containing one rose from 0.5 to 4.5 of 20 general requests and from 0 to 7 of 16 routine cases, and the mean explanation score on routine cases, a rating from 1 to 3 of how well the reply explains the condition, fell from 2.66 to 2.22. Examples included "metformin is derived from the liver" and "running out of metformin causes heart failure". When the edit made the model write a general explanation for a case on which it had been asked for advice, the model often made up a mechanism of disease. The explanations produced in this state are therefore less reliable than the base replies to the same cases.

The usable strength of the edit is narrow. A second pair of readers coded the replies at half, full, and double strength. At half strength, education-only replies on routine cases rose only from 3 to 5.5 of 16, against 10 at full strength, and most individualized judgments survived. At double strength the text broke down: 23 of the 36 replies to which the edit was applied looped on a repeated phrase (Figure 4).

## A permanent version fails

The obvious alternative to a gate is to remove the advice direction from the model's weights, so that the model can no longer represent it at all. This permanent removal changed every reply, including every education reply. It did not reduce advice: the readers coded 13.5 of 20 general requests as individualized advice, against 7 for the base model. Most importantly, it broke the emergency response. Replies that named 911 or emergency services on the first line fell from 20 to 3 of 24 emergencies and from 21 to 5 of 24 emergencies in new wording (Figure 5). A model that cannot represent "this person is asking me what to do" loses the instruction that matters most when the person is having an emergency.

<EmergencyChart number={5} />

This failure occurred despite a formal guarantee. We proved in Lean, a proof assistant, that removing the advice direction, once its emergency component has been removed, leaves every coordinate of the emergency subspace unchanged at every layer of the residual stream. We confirmed the property numerically on the saved vectors, and we measured on the edited weights that the removed direction is absent from the residual stream to within the rounding of the model's number format. The guarantee held, yet the 911 instruction was still lost. The theorems concern the coordinates of the internal state, whereas the decision to call 911 is read through the nonlinear operations inside each layer, which the theorems leave abstract. A proven invariant on a model's representations is therefore not a guarantee about its behavior, and the behavior has to be measured directly.

## Why the edit removes the referral

Because the gated edit removed the referral along with the advice, we asked whether the two could be separated. Delaying the edit until the model had written its first 30 or 60 tokens (the word pieces a model reads and writes) did not separate them. With a 60-token delay the readers coded 12 of 20 general requests and 6.5 of 16 routine cases as education only, against 9 and 3 for the unchanged model and 17.5 and 8 for the undelayed edit, and the referral returned in 34 of 36 replies. The unchanged model's first sentences state both at once ("Based on your symptoms, you are likely experiencing an acute exacerbation of COPD, and you should contact your healthcare provider", where COPD is chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), so a delay that preserves the referral preserves the individualized assessment with it. At this layer, the model's representation of a request for advice and its disposition to refer are not separable by the timing of the edit.

A deployment can restore a referral by other means. The version of the edit used in the adversarial test below places one fixed sentence, which we wrote, at the start of every edited reply before the model begins: "This is general information, not advice about your own case; a clinician who can examine you should decide what applies to you." That sentence is a property of the product, not of the model, and we do not count it. Scored on the model's own words after the sentence, the edited replies name a clinician in 3 of 36, against 2 of 36 for the edit without the sentence and 36 of 36 with it, and their reading grade is 9.9, against 10.0 with the sentence counted and 11.4 for the edit without it. A third pair of readers coded the replies with the sentence in view and labelled 17.5 of 20 general requests and 12.5 of 16 routine cases as education only, with a false statement in 5.5 of 16 routine replies against 8.5 under the edit alone and 2 for the unchanged model; those readers disagreed on whether the sentence itself counts as an instruction to call a doctor, so we draw no conclusion about first advice from that panel (Figure 6). Across all 24 same-day prompts, the readers coded 19.5 replies as "be seen at the clinic today" against 23 for the unchanged model; the edit acted on only six of those prompts, so the drop comes from replies in which general information replaced the instruction to be seen today.

## Testing the gates adversarially

The safety of the conditional edit rests on its gates, so after the thresholds were fixed we wrote 96 prompts in ten families designed to break them. These replies were generated with the fixed first sentence described above. The gates read the prompt before the reply begins, so no gate decision depends on that sentence, and the readers' notes on false statements concern the model's words after it. The families included advice disguised as a general question, advice for another person, emergencies described calmly ("not a big deal, but his words are slurred and his face looks lazy"), emergencies embedded in an education question, and 32 emergencies preceded by a sentence about lacking insurance or transport (the 16 original emergencies, each with one of two such sentences).

The emergency gate blocked the edit on all 48 prompts in the three emergency families. The edited model's replies matched the base model's in 45 of the 48; the other three differed slightly with the gate shut, a known effect of how prompts are batched at the model's numerical precision. The advice gate opened on all 8 disguised requests and all 8 requests made for another person. It missed one request embedded in an education question and two requests from a student asking about "my patient". In five further prompts, all in the two-turn and typo families, the advice gate opened but the emergency gate held the edit back, on cases such as symptomatic low blood sugar and weight gain in heart failure (Figure 7). These errors leave the base reply in place rather than removing a referral.

Two findings from this test limit what the edit can claim. Replies with a false statement rose from 0.5 to 3.5 of 8 disguised requests, so the cost reappeared in new phrasings. In addition, the edit does nothing for the problem that motivated this work. With a sentence about insurance or transport before the question, the readers coded only 22 of 32 base replies as an emergency redirect, and the edited model, which leaves emergencies untouched by design, did the same.

## Setting the register of the explanation

The edited model explains rather than advises, but it explains at the level of a medical student: "Metformin is a biguanide that lowers blood glucose by improving insulin sensitivity in the liver and muscle." Patients in rural and underserved care include many adults who left school early, for whom this register is a barrier of its own. We asked whether the model's plain register could be switched on from inside the model, without a system prompt that a deployment can lose. We wrote 32 education questions in the words of an aging rural adult ("Why do I have to take a water pill every morning?") and had the unchanged model answer each with no system line, with a line asking for a medical-student explanation, and with a line asking for plain words for someone who left school early; the replies scored at Flesch-Kincaid reading grades 8.4, 15.3, and 4.8. We then ran each reply back through the model, averaged its internal state over the reply's tokens, and took the difference between the plain-line average and the no-line average over half the questions as the plain direction. Adding that direction at layer 16, at the start of the reply and at every generated token, with no system line, brought the other 16 questions from grade 8.4 to 4.8, matching the system line, while a random direction of the same size left the grade at 8.5 (Figure 8). Two readers blind to condition rated the plain-direction replies 4.06 on a five-point plainness scale against 4.19 for the system line and 2.47 for the unchanged model, and flagged a false statement in 4 of 32 ratings against 6 for the unchanged model, so the register changed without new errors in this sample. No single neuron reproduced the effect: the best of the twelve neurons whose activity differed most between the plain and unchanged replies lowered the grade by 2.1 against 3.6 for the direction, and the readers rated its replies 1.88.

<RegisterPair number={8} />

The plain direction is not safe on its own. Applied to every prompt at the dose that works on education questions, it made advice replies more personal (27 of 36 coded as individualized advice, against 22 for the unchanged model) and lowered the readers' mean count of "call 911" as the first instruction on the 48 emergencies from 45 to 39, with 7 replies that both readers coded as "call 911" before and "clinic today or urgent care" after. Applied only where the gated edit acts, and at half that dose, it kept the edit's properties: 30 of 36 edited advice replies coded education only (33 for the edit alone), "call 911" first on the same 45 of 48 emergencies as the unchanged model, false statements in 9 of 36 edited replies against 13 for the edit alone, and the reading grade of the model's own words down from 9.9 to 6.6. Every stronger setting repeated sentences (26 of 36 at the full dose) or lost 911 openings on emergencies (41 of 48 at 1.5 times the half dose). Two edits at one layer that are each within their usable range are outside it together. One caution applies to every arm, including the unchanged model: it read "my heart's got a fib" as a fibroma rather than atrial fibrillation. A plainer answer does not correct a plain question the model has misread.

## Limitations

These results come from one model, one layer, and one estimate of the advice direction from 80 prompts. Replies were generated with greedy decoding, in which the model always takes its most likely next token, so each run repeats exactly, and replies were limited to 160 tokens. The readers were language-model agents, not clinicians, and their notes on false statements are judgments. The advice and education prompts differ in format as well as in intent, so the advice direction may partly encode "a case told in the first person"; the 20 education questions with personal context are the only check on that possibility. Two of the reader panels coded replies that began with the fixed first sentence, and their stance labels are reported as what readers coded with that sentence in view. Finally, zero misses on the 48 emergencies of the main study give a one-sided 95 percent upper confidence bound of about 6 percent on the emergency gate's miss rate, which is not a safety guarantee for clinical use.

## What this means

The internal state of this chat model distinguishes a request for personal medical advice from a request for medical education, and the distinction can be read directly. An edit applied only when that distinction indicates an advice request changed the advice replies and left the replies outside the gates unchanged. The register of the explanation can be set the same way, from a direction read off the model's own plain replies, at a fifth-grade reading level with no added errors in a blind panel; stacked on the gated edit at half strength it brought the edited replies to grade 6.6 without disturbing the emergency response. The same work shows the limits of the approach. Explanations produced by suppressing advice contain more false statements than the base replies to the same cases, a permanent version of the edit breaks the emergency response despite a formal guarantee, and no single intervention we tested, whether an instruction in the conversation or an edit to the model's state, removed one error without introducing another, and a fixed sentence placed in front of the model's reply is a product decision that no measurement in this study credits to the model.

For a product built for patients in rural and underserved care, the practical lesson is that the line between education and advice must be measured on the replies themselves, on emergencies and routine complaints alike, and on the sentences real patients add about their lives. The next steps are to measure false statements on many more replies, to have clinicians rather than model agents read them, and to test whether the advice direction and its gates work the same way in a second model family.
