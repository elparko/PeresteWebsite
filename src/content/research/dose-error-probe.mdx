Language models state medication doses in fluent, confident prose even when the dose is wrong. That risk matters most in settings where a pharmacist or physician may not be available to double-check an order, including rural primary care, where older adults with reduced kidney function and multiple concurrent drugs are common. A safety flag built into a model would help only if it catches mistakes that simpler checks miss, or if it reveals a failure mode that further training can fix.

We asked two questions using the open-weight model `Qwen3-8B`. First, does reading the model's internal state detect wrong medication doses more reliably than simply asking the model whether its own answer is correct? Second, when the model misses a dose adjustment inside a realistic clinical chart, is it missing the medical fact altogether, or does it already know the rule and fail to apply it in context?

This work studies publicly released open-weight models on research hardware. It does not test any clinical product, and nothing here should guide patient care.

## What the repaired benchmark audit uncovered

In the initial pilot, `Qwen3-8B` answered 2,391 dosing questions across 111 drugs (70.8% about patients aged 65 or older), graded against 611 machine-checkable rules tied directly to quoted passages of Food and Drug Administration (FDA) labels and DailyMed identifiers. Before launching the follow-up experiments, a programmatic audit found and fixed three flaws in the original benchmark:

1. **Removed 31 leaked paraphrase questions.** The original loader filtered the 634 paraphrase questions by question ID and rule ID, leaving 620 items. Checking each paraphrase's source question removed 31 defective items (18 implausible ages, 5 overlapping rules, 5 sex-indication mismatches, 2 prompts that stated the answer, and 1 impossible kidney-value contradiction), leaving 589 clean paraphrases.
2. **Fixed 326 mislabeled adjustment tags.** Across the 2,391 questions, 60 geriatric or weight-adjusted rules had been marked unadjusted and 266 standard or no-adjustment rules had been marked adjusted. Fixing those labels widened the error gap between adjusted and unadjusted regimens from +10.4 to +17.7 percentage points under daily-total grading, and from +8.7 to +14.5 percentage points under strict schedule grading.
3. **Strict schedule grading caught 143 hidden frequency errors.** The original grader checked only total daily dose, which let wrong schedules pass when the daily total happened to match. Requiring the stated dosing frequency to match the FDA label uncovered 143 wrong-schedule answers, raising `Qwen3-8B`'s error rate from 50.7% (1,213 of 2,391) to 56.7% (1,356 of 2,391). An independent label-reading model grader (Claude Sonnet) judged 113 of those 143 schedule errors (79.0%) clinically wrong. The most affected drug classes were opioids (47), antiepileptics (20), psychotropics (18), gastrointestinal and genitourinary drugs (18), and antiinfectives (16).

For future studies without class imbalance, we also built a balanced benchmark of 1,869 questions (3 per rule across 623 rules, 112 drugs, and 23 drug subclasses).

## Why asking the model to check itself misses wrongly refused drugs

In the pilot, reading the model's internal state right before it started typing did not beat simply asking the model whether its answer was right (0.658 versus 0.653, 95% CI -0.058 to 0.061). Reading the internal state right after the answer was written performed better, reaching 0.755 (0.101 above the self-check, 95% CI 0.039 to 0.161), and was the only signal that separated right from wrong answers within the same dosing rule (0.626 versus 0.527).

Re-testing every monitor with properly calibrated probabilities and realistic clinician review budgets (flagging only the riskiest 10% or 20% of answers; high-harm errors cover missed contraindications, overdoses, wrong schedules, and wrongly refused drugs, totaling 986 of 2,391 items or 41.2%) revealed a sharp blind spot. Whenever `Qwen3-8B` wrongly outputs `AVOID` for a drug that is actually safe to dose, its self-check almost always rubber-stamps the refusal as safe. On those wrongly refused drugs, the self-check scores 0.399 (worse than a coin flip) and catches 0.0% of them inside a 20% review budget.

Adding a calibrated internal probe closes that blind spot while achieving the highest overall accuracy:

## Two-thirds of the rules are already stored in the base model

A wrong answer on a dosing test can happen for two very different reasons: either the model never learned the FDA rule during pretraining, or it learned the rule and failed to retrieve and apply it when answering the question. To separate those two cases, we froze 384 paired screening cases (768 rows) and tested the unaligned base model (`Qwen3-8B-Base`) closed-book by measuring whether changing a patient's kidney function, liver function, or co-medication raised the base model's probability for the adjusted regimen over the unadjusted one.

| Clinical modifier type | Rules tested | Rules known by base model | Share known |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Liver impairment | 36 | 33 | 91.7% |
| Drug-drug interaction | 30 | 24 | 80.0% |
| Kidney dose reduction | 89 | 51 | 57.3% |
| Kidney contraindication (avoid) | 30 | 16 | 53.3% |
| **Total (`Qwen3-8B-Base`)** | **185** | **124** | **67.0%** |

Across 71 tested drugs, the base model already knew 124 of 185 rules (67.0%) across 46 drugs (64.8%), including more than 9 in 10 liver rules and 4 in 5 drug interactions. When the chat-tuned model gets those rules wrong, the knowledge is already in the weights; the breakdown happens in reading the clinical context and routing the adjustment into the final dose.

## Limitations

The completed pilot results cover one 8-billion-parameter model answering templated questions drawn from single FDA labels without step-by-step reasoning. Automatic grading agreed with a second label-reading model on 93.0% of answers (Cohen's kappa 0.860) and blind model audits agreed on 94 of 100 answers (kappa 0.876), while a 200-item blinded clinician audit kit is prepared and awaiting human clinical review. Held-out drug-class scores measure generalization across drug classes rather than prospective clinical performance, and an internal probe shows that error information is present, not that the model uses it to change its answer.
