Most language models pass each word through every layer once from start to finish. A looped language model works differently: it runs a shared block of middle layers several times on each token before writing the next word, spending extra computation without adding new weights. Medication dosing is a natural test of whether those extra passes actually help, because a safe order often requires combining a drug's baseline dose with a patient's kidney function, liver status, or other medications.

We tested Inkling 2 Mini (Thinking Machines Lab; 26.4 billion total parameters, routing 8 of 128 experts per token, with layers 6 through 17 run 3 times per token in a 4-bit build) on the same 2,391 FDA-label dosing questions across 111 drugs used in our `Qwen3-8B` study. At first glance, an internal error probe looked stronger after Pass 3 than after Pass 1. A closer stratified audit showed that this apparent gain came almost entirely from a single quirk of the first output token rather than better tracking of numeric dose errors.

This work evaluates publicly released research checkpoints on laboratory hardware. It does not test any clinical product and should not guide patient care.

| Looped model (Pass 1, 2, 3) | A model that runs its middle layers (here layers 6 to 17) three times in a row for each token before passing the result to the final output layers (18 to 23). |
| Internal probe | A linear classifier read off the model's hidden state after Pass 1, Pass 2, or Pass 3 to predict whether the model's final dosing answer is wrong, always tested on drug classes held out of training. |

## Dosing accuracy and how errors climb with clinical complexity

Under lenient daily-total grading, Inkling 2 Mini was wrong on 33.1% of the 2,391 questions (95% CI 28.1% to 38.3%), compared with 50.7% for `Qwen3-8B`. Missed contraindications were the largest source of error: among 401 questions where the FDA label said to avoid the drug, the model gave a dose on 279 (69.6%). In 112 of the 191 cases that had a comparable standard adult regimen, it simply stated the exact unadjusted adult dose.

Checking the dosing schedule strictly (not just the daily total) reclassified 134 answers as wrong-schedule errors, raising the overall error rate from 33.1% to 38.7%. After repairing 486 mislabeled adjustment tags in the question set, Inkling 2 Mini's error rate rose steeply with the number of clinical factors that had to be combined:

- **1-step rules** (standard adult lookup, n = 1,050): **18.7%** wrong
- **2-step rules** (indication plus one clinical modifier, n = 1,305): **44.0%** wrong
- **3-step rules** (indication plus two or more simultaneous modifiers, n = 36): **61.1%** wrong

## Why the Pass 3 probe looked better than it was

Across all 2,391 questions pooled together, an internal probe read after Pass 3 scored 0.738 compared with 0.692 after Pass 1 (a gain of +0.046, 95% CI +0.006 to +0.089; uncorrected p = 0.029, adjusted q = 0.087 across three primary tests), and did not beat the model's own self-check `P(No)` (0.738 versus 0.712, difference +0.026, 95% CI -0.016 to +0.070). Four follow-up checks revealed why that pooled Pass 3 gain did not reflect better detection of wrong doses:

### 1. Inside numeric doses, the Pass 3 gain nearly disappears

When we group every question by whether the true answer was a dose or `AVOID` and whether the model wrote a dose or `AVOID`, three of the four boxes are 100% right or 100% wrong (`avoid/avoid`: 122 right; `avoid/dose`: 279 wrong; `dose/avoid`: 50 wrong). Only one box mixes right and wrong answers: questions where the drug should be dosed and the model wrote a numeric dose (n = 1,940, with 463 errors or 23.9%).

Inside those 1,940 numeric-dose questions, the `Pass 3 minus Pass 1` probe gain drops by nearly two-thirds from +0.046 to +0.017 (95% CI -0.047 to +0.080, p = 0.653). When stratified by rule type (fixed, maximum, range, and starting doses), Pass 3 scores 0.655 (95% CI 0.580 to 0.721) and actually trails the model's own self-check `P(No)` at 0.689 (95% CI 0.619 to 0.756). Under strict schedule grading across all 2,391 questions, `P(No)` also edges out Pass 3 (0.751 versus 0.747).

### 2. Overdoses get worse from Pass 1 to Pass 3

| Error type (compared with 1,599 correct answers) | n errors | Pass 1 score | Pass 2 score | Pass 3 score | Self-check `P(No)` | Pass 3 minus Pass 1 | Pass 3 minus `P(No)` |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Overdose | 223 | 0.678 | 0.672 | 0.651 | 0.730 | -0.027 (p = 0.459) | -0.079 (p = 0.013) |
| Underdose | 236 | 0.647 | 0.619 | 0.723 | 0.669 | +0.076 (p = 0.153) | +0.054 (p = 0.089) |
| Missed contraindication | 279 | 0.738 | 0.803 | 0.814 | 0.817 | +0.076 (p = 0.007) | -0.003 (p = 0.916) |
| Wrongly avoided | 50 | 0.742 | 0.717 | 0.750 | 0.233 | +0.008 (p = 0.871) | +0.517 (p = 0.001) |

For overdoses, probe accuracy falls from 0.678 at Pass 1 to 0.651 at Pass 3 and falls behind `P(No)` (0.730) by 0.079 (p = 0.013). Just like in `Qwen3-8B`, `P(No)` fails badly when the model wrongly refuses a safe drug (0.233), whereas internal probes stay at 0.750.

### 3. The single first-token split that drove the anticoagulant jump

In anticoagulants (n = 217), the probe jumped from 0.507 at Pass 1 to 0.786 at Pass 3 (+0.279). Looking at the very first token the model writes after `FINAL:` explains the entire jump. In every single `AVOID` reply (172 of 172), the first token is `" AV"`. In every single dose reply (2,219 of 2,219), the first token is a plain space `" "` before the number.

On the 64 anticoagulant questions where the true label is `AVOID` (35 missed versus 29 correct), being wrong is 100% identical to starting the reply with `" "` instead of `" AV"`. On those 64 questions, the probe flips from 0.299 at Pass 1 to 0.947 at Pass 3 (+0.648, p = 0.001) simply because Pass 3 is the only pass that feeds directly into the final output layers where the model commits to `" "` versus `" AV"`. Dropping the 401 `AVOID` questions from the dataset reduces the overall `Pass 3 minus Pass 1` gain to +0.015 (95% CI -0.043 to +0.072, p = 0.669).

### 4. Within-rule ranking and a calibration bug fixed

Across the 170 dosing rules where Inkling got some patients right and others wrong (693 questions), Pass 1 and Pass 2 probes ranked errors within the same rule near chance (0.553 and 0.544), rising to 0.632 at Pass 3 overall and 0.594 inside numeric doses. The audit also caught a cross-fold scaling bug in layers 0 to 5 (where mixing uncalibrated raw probe scores across folds dragged the combined score to 0.631 even though every individual layer scored 0.671 to 0.681), which is now fixed with per-fold probability calibration.

## Next test: holding output distance fixed across 1 to 5 passes (Study 3)

Three design limits in the fixed 3-pass model prevented a clean test of whether extra passes genuinely improve multi-step dosing: only Pass 3 fed the final output layers; the recorded layer outputs reset every 6 layers rather than capturing the cumulative state read by the output head; and a 2,048-token cap cut off 167 of 300 chain-of-thought runs before the model finished answering.

> 1. Whether running 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 passes improves 2-step and 3-step dosing accuracy on drugs the model already knows on 1-step lookups.
> 2. A compute-matched comparison of silent extra passes (`k = 5`, no written reasoning) against full written reasoning (`k = 1` and `k = 3`, with an 8,192-token budget so answers are not cut off).
> 3. Surgical state-swapping tests on the drug name, the patient's lab value, and the final question span to trace which pass retrieves each fact and which pass combines them.
> 4. Calibrated error probes taken directly from the final pre-output state at every pass count (`k = 1` to `5`), holding distance to the output strictly fixed.

## Limitations

The completed Inkling 2 Mini results cover a single 4-bit model answering templated FDA-label questions without written reasoning. Differences between Inkling 2 Mini and `Qwen3-8B` reflect model size, training data, architecture, and quantization together, not looping alone. All stratified audit numbers are deterministic re-analyses of the saved pilot outputs; testing whether extra passes causally compute multi-step adjustments awaits Study 3.
