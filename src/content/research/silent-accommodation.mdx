Real patients rarely describe symptoms in a vacuum. Especially in rural and underserved communities, people add context about their lives: they have no insurance, they live 85 miles from the nearest hospital, they do not want to question their doctor, or they feel fine and do not want to go in. In an earlier pilot on `Qwen3.5-4B`, adding one sentence about lacking insurance lowered how often the model opened with "call EMS" on an initial set of 16 emergencies, though that effect did not repeat on 32 new scenarios and a neutral sentence about reaching care changed replies by a similar amount.

That mixed result left a sharper question. When we hold prompt length strictly identical by pairing every patient-circumstance sentence with a neutral sentence of the exact same token count, do language models systematically back off from emergency 911 advice or stop-medication warnings? Does that habit grow stronger or weaker as models get larger, from 1.7 billion to 35 billion parameters? And when a model downgrades a recommendation, does its written reasoning admit why, or does it quietly rationalize a softer answer on clinical grounds?

This study tests open-weight models on controlled synthetic clinical scenarios. It does not evaluate any deployed clinical tool and should not guide patient care.

<summary><h2 id="terms-used-here">Terms used here</h2></summary>

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Token-matched control sentence | A neutral sentence (such as describing a garden window or a hallway clock) written to take the exact same number of tokens as the patient-circumstance sentence, so any difference in the reply cannot come from prompt length alone. |
| Silent accommodation | Backing off from an urgent safety instruction (such as calling 911 or stopping a dangerous drug) because of a non-clinical detail like cost or reluctance, without stating that detail as the reason in the reasoning trace. |
| Safety rank | A numeric score measuring how strongly a reply meets the required clinical safety action (higher means a clearer emergency or stop-medication directive). |
| Thinking block (`<think>`) | The step-by-step reasoning text a Qwen3 model writes to itself before producing the reply shown to the user. |
| Internal monitor | A classifier read from the model's hidden internal state (at the end of the extra sentence, the end of thinking, or the start of the reply) to detect safety drops even when the visible reasoning looks clinically sound. |

</details>

## How the test is built

The benchmark consists of 180 clinically clear scenarios (split evenly into 90 discovery scenarios for fitting monitors and 90 held-out test scenarios) across three balanced groups:

- **Acute emergencies (60 scenarios):** textbook emergencies where the safe answer is to call 911 immediately and not drive.
- **High-alert medication warnings (60 scenarios):** dangerous regimens or contraindications where the safe answer is to stop or not take the drug right away.
- **Routine questions (60 scenarios):** everyday non-urgent complaints and medical education questions, included as a guardrail check so any safety intervention cannot cheat by sending everyone to the emergency room.

Every scenario is asked 13 ways (2,340 prompts per model): once with no extra sentence, 6 times with a sentence about a non-clinical circumstance (cost and lack of insurance, 85-mile rural distance, informal dialect, a doctor who dislikes being questioned, patient pushback, or being an 84-year-old retired farmer), and 6 times with a matched control sentence verified to have the exact same token length. Every reply gets a generous 4,096-token budget so models finish thinking cleanly rather than getting cut off mid-sentence, and we save the model's internal state at three checkpoints along each reply.

We are running six sizes of the Qwen3 family in sequence: `Qwen3-1.7B`, `Qwen3-4B`, `Qwen3-8B`, `Qwen3-14B`, `Qwen3-32B`, and `Qwen3.6-35B-A3B`.

## First results from `1.7B` and `4B`

Raising the reply budget to 4,096 tokens virtually eliminated cut-off thinking blocks: 98.2% of `Qwen3-1.7B` replies (all 2,340 completed) and 99.2% of `Qwen3-4B` replies (1,920 of 2,340 scored in an interim check) finished normally, with only 1.6% and 0.7% cut off. Both models stayed accurate on routine questions without false alarms (97.2% to 98.6% on `1.7B`, and 99.4% to 100.0% on `4B`).

Even in the first step up in model size from `1.7B` to `4B`, a length-matched gap already opens on emergency triage:

On `Qwen3-1.7B`, the emergency safety rank barely differed between patient-circumstance sentences and matched neutral sentences (1.31 versus 1.35, a difference of -0.04). On the interim `Qwen3-4B` slice, the safety rank rose to 2.54 on neutral sentences (almost identical to the 2.57 baseline with no extra sentence), then dropped to 2.12 when a patient-circumstance sentence of the exact same length was present (a drop of 0.43). Yet when asked directly whether the case was safe to downgrade, `Qwen3-4B`'s self-check probability stayed near zero (0.000 on neutral sentences and 0.019 on circumstance sentences).

Broken down by sentence type on the interim `4B` slice, the steepest drops in meeting the full safety standard came from doctor-authority pressure (-8.3 percentage points on both emergencies and medication warnings), cost and lack of insurance (-6.7 points on emergencies), patient pushback (-6.7 points on medication warnings), and older age (-5.0 points on emergencies), while mentioning an 85-mile rural drive moved in the opposite direction (+13.3 points on emergencies).
